## What it claims

Kyle Poyar wrote *Growth Unhinged* — a B2B newsletter on SaaS growth, pricing, and go-to-market —
for **more than four and a half years while employed full-time as a VC operating partner**, during
which he was **contractually barred from monetizing it at all**. He went solo in **September 2025**.
Within roughly ten months he is pacing at **$1.5M annual revenue** across five business lines.

His central monetization argument: **in a niche B2B audience, CPM and cost-per-click pricing are
irrelevant and drastically underprice the inventory.** What matters is the advertiser's ROI, and if
your readers are decision-makers with budget, that ROI can justify almost any rate.

## Specific numbers and facts

### The business
| | |
|---|---|
| Newsletter subscribers | **85,000** |
| LinkedIn followers | **110,000** |
| Revenue pace (2026) | **$1.5M** |
| Business lines | **5, each clearing ≥$100k** |
| Paid subscriptions | **$125k run rate** |
| Advertising | **5-6x subscriptions** (so roughly $625-750k) |
| Time solo at recording | **~10 months** |

The five lines: **advertising, paid subscriptions, consulting, speaking, podcasting.**

### ⚠️ The part that matters most for us: he built it while employed
- Started posting on **LinkedIn in March 2020**; launched the **newsletter in March 2021**.
- Wrote it for **4.5+ years alongside a full-time VC job**, with **no monetization permitted** —
  his employer didn't allow a side business.
- **Went solo September 2025.** That same week he emailed one of the partners who had been asking
  to advertise, quoted a price, and they **locked three quarters in advance for over $100,000** —
  closed without a call.
- He **had not yet incorporated.** He formed the LLC, opened a bank account, and figured out how to
  invoice *after* the deal was agreed.
- He says he **underpriced**: "I should have charged 50% more."

**The audience was fully built before any monetization existed.** The revenue arrived within days
of him deciding to accept it.

### Newsletter growth curve (slow, then not)
- **~2,000 subscribers within a couple of weeks** — from announcing on LinkedIn and Twitter to an
  audience that already existed.
- Then **~18 months to reach 5,000.** He calls the pace "glacial."
- Then **5,000 → 15-20,000**, and steady compounding year over year since.
- **The single biggest inflection was moving from biweekly to weekly cadence.**

### LinkedIn as the top-of-funnel engine
From his post *"LinkedIn isn't over. You're just bad at it"*: **+62% YoY to 90,000+ followers,
14M impressions in a year, $0 spent.** (That post also generated more unsubscribes than anything
else he's written — the subject line offended people.)

His six-point playbook:
1. Start by engaging with other people's posts
2. Focus on a great graphic
3. Write zero-click content — don't stress about the link
4. Post 2-3x per week; **Sundays work surprisingly well**
5. Find your counterintuitive POV
6. Repurpose your greatest hits

Two newer changes he learned from LinkedIn's own creator masterclasses:
- **Profile relevance drives the algorithm.** Keep your profile current and aligned to what you
  post about, so the algorithm treats you as the expert on that topic.
- **Write a "maximalist opening."** Establish credibility and summarize what's coming *up front* to
  earn the "read more" click. Not a slow draw, not a misdirect, not a two-word hot take — which he
  admits feels like bragging and is uncomfortable to write.

**"Cringe mountain":** when you start posting, friends message you asking what you've become, and
that lasts a couple of months. Covid gave him the conviction to push through. His point: *you learn
to summit by summiting* — the skill is only acquirable by doing it publicly.

### Graphics are "the secret behind my growth"
- Built in **Google Slides** (with a little Canva) from a template — fixed color scheme, two fonts
  (a handwritten one, likely Covered By Your Grace, plus Spartan).
- **Name and logo at the bottom of every graphic**, so when they get stolen — which good ones
  always are — the source travels with them.
- **Anti-polish on purpose.** Overly designed, glossy work reads as corporate marketing and gets
  skipped. Slightly raw and hand-done performs better.
- **Pyramid principle** (from six years in consulting): the graphic should be readable in 5 seconds,
  30 seconds, *and* 5 minutes.
- **Deliberately not "airport simple."** Density drives **dwell time**, which the algorithms reward.
  His most popular graphic is **40 company logos with their pricing model in small text** — an AI
  pricing landscape he updates quarterly. Not beautiful; endlessly useful. **Sankey diagrams**
  ("the ones that look like rivers") also do "crazy crazy well" — visual novelty plus long dwell.
- The bar: something people forward to a colleague or drop in a Slack channel.

### ⚠️ Ad pricing — the most transferable idea in the interview
- **Reject CPM/CPC for niche B2B.** "If one of my ad partners can sell a $100,000 deal after a
  quarter of advertising, they've made a great ROI independent of cost per thousand impressions."
- Sells **quarterly packages**, not impressions. Each includes:
  - Newsletter ad inventory
  - **Category exclusivity** — competitors blocked while your ads run (valuable to VC-backed
    companies fighting for a category)
  - **He writes the ad copy himself, in his own voice** — better performance, lighter lift for the
    advertiser
- **Scarcity is engineered.** One ad slot per issue: "once I've sold that inventory, I can't resell
  it — there's a butt on the seat." He **deliberately constrains inventory**, sells out, and only
  then either raises prices or releases more. Explicit airline-yield analogy.
- **Selectivity increases pricing power**, because the advertiser's ROI depends on their product
  actually being good: "if their product is great and my audience really wants to buy it, that ad
  partner sees a fantastic ROI and I don't have to write good content at all."
- **Partnerships now run a year**, with deliverables spread across it.

### Ads didn't damage the audience
He waited **4.5 years before running a single ad** and was "nervous as hell." He placed it above the
content, disclosed it, kept it visually separate, and made no announcement. Result: **no measurable
change in open rates, engagement, or unsubscribes.** Readers actively wrote in to say they'd bought
products they discovered there.

**What works: not the hard sell.** Nobody buys $100k billing software off one ad. What works is
**thematic alignment plus an educational offer** — if the issue is about AI pricing, the billing
company's webinar fits. One ad drove **500 webinar signups → hundreds of thousands in pipeline.**
"You've got to date them before they'll accept your marriage proposal."

### The free/paid line
> **"The newsletter should be free. If somebody is reading it on their phone on the way to work,
> that should be free. If they are applying the lessons, they should pay for that."**

- **Free:** casual, informational, read in five minutes on a commute.
- **Paid:** original research that drives a business decision — e.g. a 60-page PDF report.
  His framing: the alternative is **paying Gartner or Forrester tens of thousands** for comparable
  analysis.
- **⚠️ He names a real structural tension:** ads need maximum free reach; subscriptions need gated
  value. **The two business lines actively compete**, and he calls balancing them the trickiest
  thing he does.

### The consulting flywheel
Splits his time **~50/50 between media and consulting**. Media attracts consulting clients;
consulting tells him what companies are actually struggling with right now, which makes the writing
better — *"it's like I'm in the room with my readers."* He also says the hands-on work is what keeps
imposter syndrome at bay.

### Content strategy: build for shelf life
Write **comprehensive, canonical, deliberately dense guides** — past email length limits — that
people **save, return to, and share months later.** Harder to produce, but the shelf life means the
writing **compounds** instead of resetting each week. He also runs a referral program.

**Research process:** test an idea on LinkedIn → watch who engages and DMs → talk to those people →
write the deep newsletter piece → promote it back on LinkedIn → sometimes a follow-up post. He also
surveys readers directly (a couple hundred responded on how they use Claude Code).

His framing: **treat audience and content as equal priorities.** Not "spend 80% of your time making
something great, then push it out and hope."

### Collaboration over competition
Bylines swapped with **Lenny Rachitsky**, **Emily Kramer** (marketing/startups), **CJ Gustafson**
(finance/startups); mutual cross-promotion. *"You might have competitors in other writers, but
really no one sees themselves as a competitor"* — a reader who likes his work will like theirs too.

### What he thinks could go wrong
- **Dropping the ball on deliverables.** An ad-and-consulting business means selling *and*
  delivering — events, webinars, speaking, joint appearances. It adds up and demands real project
  management.
- **Shiny object syndrome.** He wants to add a community, first-party events, and a second podcast,
  and worries he'll lose sight of what got him here. He'd already said five business lines is
  probably too many.

### What he wants in three years — explicitly not growth
Having hit financial stability, he says he has **no ambition to maximize revenue**. He wants
sustainability, a strong audience connection, learning, **more than six weeks of vacation**, and to
work from somewhere more interesting than Boston. *"I'm a solopreneur to be able to enjoy life and
have more flexibility, not so that I can work 100-hour weeks."*

He does offer one direct recommendation: **if you're torn, take the B2B path.**

## What's useful for this project

**1. ⚠️ This is the first source whose build phase actually fits our capacity constraint.** Fuller
was deploying millions; Imran went full-time at 20-50 hrs/week. **Poyar built an 85,000-subscriber
audience and a 110,000-follower LinkedIn presence entirely alongside a demanding full-time job, over
4.5 years, earning nothing from it.** That is a nights-and-weekends shape. It also sets the honest
expectation: **the timeline is years, and the monetization comes at the end, not along the way.**

**2. The revenue-per-subscriber gap is enormous and it's about the niche.**
- Imran: **1.7M followers → ~$250k** (~$0.15/follower/yr)
- Poyar: **85,000 subscribers → ~$1.5M** (~$17/subscriber/yr)

**Over 100x difference in revenue per audience member.** Poyar's audience is 20x smaller and earns
6x more. This is the single most decision-relevant number pair we have, and it argues hard for a
narrow, high-value B2B niche — directly relevant to the Track A vs Track B comparison.

**3. Price on the advertiser's ROI, not on impressions.** The clearest transferable mechanic here.
Combined with engineered scarcity (one slot, sell out, then raise) and category exclusivity, it
explains most of the gap above. Worth treating as a candidate principle once corroborated.

**4. Weekly cadence was the growth inflection.** Not a viral moment — a schedule change. Cheap to
test and directly actionable.

**5. Distribution platform ≠ home platform.** LinkedIn is top-of-funnel, idea-testing lab, and
research channel; the newsletter is where depth and ownership live. A concrete division of labor,
and another instance of the owned-audience theme — now **three sources, three routes to it.**

**6. The free/paid tension is real and unresolved.** He's running both and says they fight. Any
strategy combining ads with paid subscriptions inherits this problem — worth carrying into Phase 5
rather than discovering it late.

**7. Depth compounds; volume resets.** Canonical, dense, save-worthy pieces keep working for months.
For a 5-10 hr/week operator who cannot win on volume, **this is the strategically important claim in
the whole interview** — it suggests the constraint may favor a depth strategy rather than merely
tolerating one.

**8. Expertise-led is the model here** — six years consulting, then VC. This is a third data point
in the expertise question, and it lands on Fuller's side: his edge is subject-matter authority plus
proprietary research, not format iteration.

## Caveats

- **Self-reported revenue** with no documentation. The $1.5M is a **run-rate extrapolation** — he
  says so plainly: "I'm just literally taking what I'm doing right now and extending it out for a
  year." **It is not a completed year.**
- **⚠️ Beehiiv conflict.** The host is a beehiiv employee on a beehiiv-owned podcast interviewing a
  creator currently in a paid beehiiv partnership. Disclosed on air, but relevant.
- **He is ~10 months into monetization.** The ad concentration (5-6x subscriptions) has not been
  tested through a downturn in B2B marketing budgets — historically the first line cut. His
  business is more concentrated than the five-line framing suggests.
- **The paid subscription line is small and new** ($125k, ~7-8 months). The "five business lines
  each clearing $100k" framing flatters a business that is substantially one ad business.
- **Enormous unpriced advantage: the VC job.** Four years as an operating partner at a VC firm
  supplied the network, the credibility, the proprietary data, and the relationships that became
  advertisers. The advertisers were *already asking to buy* before he had anything to sell. **A
  reader starting without that network is not running the same experiment**, and this is the single
  biggest thing the interview doesn't examine.
- **Survivorship bias persists.** We don't know how many B2B newsletters ground away for 18 months
  at a "glacial" pace and simply stopped — which is exactly what his own curve looked like before it
  turned.
- **Platform tactics are perishable.** The LinkedIn algorithm advice (profile relevance, maximalist
  openings) is current as of mid-2026 and will not age well. Verify before acting.
- **Unresolved and worth resolving:** he says selectivity in ad partners *increases* pricing power,
  and separately that a great partner product means "I don't have to write good content at all."
  Those sit oddly together — the second undercuts the trust-based argument the rest of the
  interview rests on.
