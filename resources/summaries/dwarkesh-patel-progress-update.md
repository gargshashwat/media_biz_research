# Dwarkesh Podcast Progress Update

> **Topic:** **AI, history, economics, and science** — long-form interviews with researchers and
> scholars. Notable for preparation depth (~2 weeks per guest) rather than access or celebrity.

- **Source:** **Dwarkesh Patel, writing about his own business** — first-person, not an interview
- **Type:** Blog post / public progress update
- **Link:** https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/progress-update
- **Published:** 2024-08-20 · **Consumed:** 2026-08-26
- **Trust:** **high for candour, low for numbers.** This is someone thinking aloud about his own business with no product to sell and no interviewer to impress — he leads with how close he came to quitting. But it contains **almost no figures**: no revenue, no subscriber count, no ad rates. ⚠️ **Two years old**, and he says himself he was about to change strategy.

> **Feeds:** Phase 1 (podcast format), Phase 3 (case studies), Phase 2.

---

## What it claims

The central admission:

> **"I was 2 months away from quitting the podcast for 2 years."**

For roughly two years he repeatedly planned to wind the podcast down and go build a GPT wrapper
instead. Each time, one more scheduled interview deferred the decision.

---

## The specifics

### It was not obviously promising to anyone, including him
- After college he had no job lined up. **Anil Varanasi — one of a few hundred listeners at the time
  — asked what it would cost him to do this full-time for six months. He said $10,000**, which he
  considered a highball since he was living with his parents in Texas.
- **Anil later told him, half-jokingly, that the grant was one of his biggest regrets** — that it had
  caused Dwarkesh to waste his talents on a tiny podcast instead of something ambitious. (He's since
  changed his mind.)
- A blog post that **got Jeff Bezos to say nice things** bought a few more months of not quitting.
- A second small grant from Effective Ideas extended the runway again.

**⚠️ The most successful research podcast of its cohort was, for two years, a thing its own creator
and his earliest backer thought was a mistake.**

### Guest selection — the counterintuitive part
> "I choose guests not based on whether I want to spend two hours chatting with them, but **whether I
> would learn a lot by spending two weeks preparing.** It's a high bar, and it's often uncorrelated
> with how famous they are."

- **His most popular guest was Sarah Paine**, a historian he describes as "an obscure intellectual and
  a hidden gem."
- Episodes with his friends **Trenton, Sholto, and Leopold beat out CEOs of famous companies.**
- The Trenton/Sholto episode he expected to flop for being too technical **went viral** and became
  "Dwarkesh Podcast canon."

His read on why: **"people want to be treated to the conversation that I would actually have with
them at dinner."** He thinks the "let's back up and explain this for the audience" move is
*"annoying and patronizing"*, and that immersion learning is more effective and more fun. (Notably,
a commenter disagrees directly underneath.)

### The compounding-preparation model
He quotes Bezos on quarterly results being baked three years earlier, then applies it:

> "A great episode is baked in thanks to the reading I was doing **6 months ago.** Right now I'm
> digging the rabbit holes which will pay dividends during the interview I record 6 months from now.
> It's always very valuable to keep a bunch of **background processes** running."

He'd been reading WWII and military history for months with no episode in mind before asking Twitter
who to interview — which produced Sarah Paine, his biggest episode.

### ⚠️ On why most podcasters don't get smarter
> "There are many podcasters who have been interviewing experts in every domain for years and years.
> **And they don't seem that much smarter as a result.** You can't just hope to become some
> giga-generalist by chatting up a bunch of experts. **The default path for a podcaster is that you
> don't learn that much over the years** and spend most of your time on topics which are easy to talk
> about in a shallow way (corruption of institutions, culture war, etc)."

His counter-strategy: **write more.** He says the AI interviews he did after publishing his scaling
post were far better than the ones before it, and floats replacing broad pre-reading with *"spend two
weeks writing my take on the most interesting question in the guest's field, then ask them to explain
why I'm a total moron."*

He names **Tyler Cowen as the exception that proves the rule** — the best interview podcast, because
most of his job is reading, writing, and forming takes.

### The advertising problem — a genuinely useful piece of monetization reasoning
Ads were "quite lucrative" and more so than he expected. But:

> Selling and vetting the ad, writing a bespoke script, refining copy, recording, re-recording on
> feedback, and adding graphic overlays = **"close to a day+ of work per ad."**

Which led him to:

> "If ads are going to take this much time anyway, wouldn't it be better to use that time to make
> content for paid subscribers? **The very reason these advertisers want to sponsor my podcast is
> because I have a great audience with lots of potential users. But what product could possibly have
> better audience fit on my podcast than paid subscriptions for my Substack?**"

He frames the ad time as a **deadweight loss to his audience** and paid subscriptions as a hedge
worth taking even if initially less profitable.

### Operations and the "flying monkeys" problem
- Pace at the time: **one episode every two weeks**, which he considered slow for a full-time job.
- Less than half his to-do list was reading or writing. The rest — selling ads, micromanaging clips
  and edits, shoot logistics, follow-ups — he calls **"flying monkeys."**
- His estimate: **offload all of it and he could record two episodes a week**, or one plus real
  writing time.
- **His editor was an incoming college sophomore** working over summer break, and he credits him with
  the difference between one episode a fortnight and one a month.
- **Higher frequency would let him take more risk on obscure guests** — a dud costs two weeks of
  preparation at his pace.
- **Urgency:** more than once an elderly scholar he vaguely planned to interview died before he sent
  the cold email — most recently James Scott.

### On wasted time
> "Wasted time is measured not in afternoons spent dithering around, skimming books and taking
> leisurely walks. **Wasted time is measured in weeks spent shoveling bullshit, answering emails,
> micromanaging the insignificant.** Wasted time is measured in the months spent working on an
> ill-considered and inferior project."

He also hired **three friends as personal tutors** (econ, hardware, physics) and notes the curriculum
they compile is as valuable as the sessions.

## What's useful for this project

**1. ⚠️ The single best antidote to survivorship bias we have.** Every other source narrates a
trajectory backwards. This one is written from *inside* the uncertainty, by someone who thought he
was failing, whose first backer thought the grant was a mistake, and who nearly quit for two years.
**Anyone reading the other case studies should read this one immediately after.**

**2. Preparation as the differentiator — and it compounds on a long lag.** Reading done six months
ago produces today's episode. **For a 5-10 hr/week operator this is unusually encouraging: depth
accumulated slowly in the background is exactly the kind of asset that doesn't require weekly
intensity.** It aligns with the depth-over-volume finding from Poyar, Lenny, and beehiiv — now four
sources.

**3. Obscure guests beat famous ones.** Empirically, in his own analytics. This undercuts the
assumption that a podcast needs access or a network to work, and is the strongest available argument
that **preparation can substitute for status** — directly relevant to whether a Track A podcast is
viable without an existing network.

**4. ⚠️ The ad-vs-subscription time calculation is a real and underrated cost.** A day of work per ad
read is invisible in every "just get sponsors" recommendation. **This is the counterweight to
Poyar's ads-over-subscriptions argument, and both can be true**: Poyar sells quarterly packages at
high prices to few advertisers; Dwarkesh does bespoke work per ad. **The lesson is that ad
monetization scales with how you structure the offer, not just with audience size.**

**5. "The default path for a podcaster is that you don't learn that much"** is worth carrying into
any format decision. The format doesn't confer the expertise — the preparation does.

**6. The "flying monkeys" ratio should inform the whole plan.** He was full-time and less than half
his time was on the actual work. **At 5-10 hrs/week, if the same ratio held, the real output would be
2-5 hours.** Any format chosen should be judged on its overhead-to-craft ratio, not just its
production time. **Podcasts look bad on this measure** — booking, logistics, editing, ad ops.

## Caveats

- **⚠️ Almost no numbers.** No revenue, subscriber count, download figures, or ad rates. Useful for
  reasoning, useless for modelling.
- **⚠️ Two years old (August 2024)** and explicitly forward-looking — he was *considering* paid
  subscriptions and *planning* to write more. **We don't know what he actually did.** His business
  today is unstudied, and this should be treated as a snapshot of intent, not outcome.
- **He was full-time**, funded by grants, living with his parents at zero expenses. The freedom to
  spend two weeks preparing one interview is not available part-time.
- **Enormous latent advantages go unexamined** — a blog post that reached Jeff Bezos, friends who are
  frontier AI researchers (Sholto, Trenton, Leopold), and an SF network. His "obscure guests beat
  famous ones" finding is partly a story about having remarkable friends.
- **The immersion-over-explanation claim is contested in his own comments** and is a stylistic bet,
  not a validated principle.
