## Verified facts (FT / TechCrunch via Wikipedia)

- **Born 1996.** Enrolled at King's College London to study law and **dropped out after four weeks.**
- **Started The Twenty Minute VC with $50 and not a single contact in venture capital.**
  **First episode: Guy Kawasaki, released 10 January 2015** — he was 18.
- **May 2017:** left Atomico after six months. **2018:** co-founded Stride.VC with Fred Destin.
- **May 2020:** launched the 20VC fund. **June 2021:** raised a further **$140M**.
- **October 2024: raised $400M for his third fund** — split **$125M seed / $275M Series A.**
- **As of 2024 the firm manages over $600M.** LPs reportedly include MIT's endowment, RIT Capital
  Partners, and 40+ unicorn founders.
- Portfolio includes **Linktree, Linear, Sorare, Tripledot, NexHealth, Captions.ai, Merge, Pachama.**

## How it was built

### ⚠️ Four years before a single dollar
> "For three years, Harry didn't get 1,000 plays per episode. **For four years, didn't earn a
> dollar.**"

**This is the most important number in the case study**, and it is consistent with everything else in
this research ([L13](../../LEARNINGS.md)).

### ⚠️ Two years interviewing only seed investors in San Francisco
His stated biggest mistake is **trying to be too much to too many people**, and his fix was extreme
narrowing:

> "For two years, Harry **only interviewed seed investors in San Francisco.** He knew his guest. He
> knew his audience. Nothing else. **Interviewing 'entrepreneurs' is the worst podcast idea because
> it's way too broad.**"

His illustration of a *good* podcast concept: *"people who scaled a newsletter from zero to 1 million
in 12 months. Start it today. Two people a week."*

Narrow focus gives you a **clear guest profile, an obvious sponsorship model, and a thousand true
fans.** His instruction: **"Narrow until it hurts."**

### The cold-email system that got the guests
For Guy Kawasaki with zero standing: **found his email in the source code of his website**, read
three of his books, and sent four lines —

> "Hi Guy. Keeping short out of respect for your time. I've read all your books. In X book, page 284,
> you said X. I would love to discuss this with you on my podcast. I know you've got a book tour
> coming — could we align it? Let me know, Harry."

The structure: **four lines max · proof of work · clear ask · an esoteric PS** (something few people
know about the guest). **He emailed Marc Benioff 53 times, with a different PS each time** — his
holiday home, his favourite whiskey and vintage — before getting a yes.

### Media → access → trust → capital
Founders he'd interviewed started sending him deals. He had no money. Then one multi-billionaire
founder offered: *"Take money from me and invest it."*

**He went to ~50 other founders from the show and raised $8M in about a week**, at $100-250K each.

> **Media created access. Access created trust. Trust created capital.**

Then **$8M → $140M → $400M.** He describes **$8M → $140M as the hardest transition** — moving from
individual to institutional capital, which required proving access to the best founders.

### The product he sells founders is distribution
> "Most VCs sell knowledge or network. **He sells a product founders actually want: media and
> distribution.**"

Concrete claims: **one LinkedIn post for portfolio company Fixer generated $3-4M in ARR within 72
hours**; another portfolio company received thousands of job applications from a single post. The
Linktree founder **slid into his Twitter DMs** — a multi-million-dollar gain from a DM.

**20VC now has a 21-person media team** — video, short form, long form, LinkedIn.

### On competition
> "VCs don't create content because you need personality. **That removes 80% of them.**"

And his geographic arbitrage: in Silicon Valley you compete with Sequoia, Benchmark and a16z; in
Europe *"Harry can't name five good Series A funds."* **Choose weak competition.**

### Transparency as positioning
He passed on Deel at seed — a $250K cheque at a $12M valuation from an $8M fund, which he calculates
cost him **~$115M in personal carry.** He also passed on Vanta. **He raises both in interviews
constantly rather than burying them.** Related: in the $400M fundraise deck he included a slide of
**reasons not to invest**, led with the hardest question ("can we get 15%+ ownership?"), and answered
it with data showing his last seven deals averaged 12.5%, up 50% year over year.

## What's useful for this project

**1. ⚠️ This is now our single best-documented instance of [L6](../../LEARNINGS.md) — the money is in
the adjacent business.** The podcast has never been the revenue. It is the sourcing, diligence, and
winning mechanism for a fund that manages $600M+. Fuller bought magazines to sell real estate and
aircraft financing; Stebbings runs a podcast to source deals. **Same structure, different asset.**

**2. ⚠️ Stebbings and Lenny flatly contradict each other on niching — and this matters for D1.**
Stebbings: *"narrow until it hurts."* Lenny: *"if I followed the classic advice of picking a narrow
niche I'd have focused on just product management, but I don't care about product management that
much."* Both built enormous audiences.

**A possible reconciliation (my inference, not stated by either):** *narrow to start, broaden to
sustain.* Stebbings narrowed hard for two years then broadened; Lenny began with Airbnb-specific
growth and marketplace research before widening. **Narrow buys you a clear guest/reader profile and a
sellable sponsorship story when you have no leverage; breadth buys you the will to keep going for
six years.** Recorded as an open contradiction rather than resolved.

**3. The cold-email structure is directly usable and costs nothing.** Four lines, demonstrated proof
of work, one clear ask, an esoteric PS — plus a willingness to send it 53 times. **For someone
without a network this is the concrete mechanism the Dwarkesh case implies but never spells out.**

**4. Four years to the first dollar.** Add to [L13](../../LEARNINGS.md). Every case in this research
now has a multi-year unpaid runway, and this is the longest.

**5. "Choose weak competition" is a real strategic input for Phase 4.** Stebbings' European arbitrage
rhymes with Rowan Cheung's *"verticalized content is blue ocean"* and Fuller being told freight was
"a boring niche" by every VC he pitched. **Three sources now argue that the absence of good
incumbents is itself the opportunity** — which is a different and more actionable filter than market
size.

## Caveats

- **⚠️ Two-tier sourcing.** Fund facts are FT/TechCrunch-grade. **The tactics, quotes and anecdotes
  come from a secondary newsletter retelling a podcast interview**, and I could not access the
  original. Treat the quotes as approximately-what-he-said.
- **⚠️ The source contains unevidenced pseudo-science** about founder quality signals which I have
  excluded. Its presence lowers my confidence in the rest of the piece's rigour.
- **Media→capital does not generalize.** It works because in venture, reputation *is* the product and
  it converts directly into fund economics. **In most niches there is no equivalent conversion.**
  Note also that a fund earns management fees and carry — a business model entirely unavailable to a
  media operator without an investable strategy.
- **The 21-person media team** is the current state. Whatever the playbook is now, it is not a
  one-person operation, and the tactics described are from the era when it was.
- **Started at 18, full-time, with parental support implied**, in a podcast market with far less
  competition in 2015. **He also dropped out of university to do it** — the risk profile is not
  comparable to a 5-10 hr/week side project.
- **The $3-4M ARR from one LinkedIn post is an unverified single-source claim** and exactly the kind
  of number that grows in retelling.
- **Survivorship, acutely.** Passing on Deel and Vanta are framed as endearing scars. Most people who
  spend four years earning nothing on a podcast do not end up managing $600M.
