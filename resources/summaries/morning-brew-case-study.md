# Morning Brew — case study

> **Topic:** **Business and financial news, made digestible and funny**, for young professionals.
> Started as a WSJ summary for finance students. **Explicitly the non-expert, non-B2B counter-case**
> to Poyar, Lenny and Fuller — though, as below, **it did not stay non-B2B.**

- **Type:** ⚠️ **Compiled case study, not a single resource.** Synthesized from multiple secondary sources because no single good primary resource was available.
- **Compiled:** 2026-08-25 (research done by Claude at the user's request)
- **Trust:** **medium.** Revenue and subscriber figures recur consistently across independent outlets and trade press (Adweek, Axios, CNBC, Press Gazette), which is better corroboration than any single-source interview we've studied. But most growth-tactic write-ups are content marketing by referral/newsletter SaaS vendors retelling the same founder anecdotes. **The numbers are solid; the tactical narratives are third-hand and flattering.**

## ⚠️ The headline finding: it didn't stay a consumer business

Morning Brew was studied as the consumer, no-expertise counter-example. It is that — but the ending
undercuts the premise:

- **B2B division revenue: under $5M (2020) → ~$25M (2024).**
- **In 2025, Morning Brew's B2B division was expected to surpass the revenue of its flagship
  consumer newsletter for the first time.**
- In February 2025, co-founder Austin Rief stepped down as CEO and was replaced by **Robert Dippell,
  whose background is B2B media** (Bisnow, Omeda).

**The most successful consumer newsletter of its generation migrated toward B2B economics as it
matured, and then hired a B2B operator to run it.** The reason given is the one every source in this
project keeps arriving at: professional audiences command far higher ad rates, because *"a finance
software company wants a room of the exact buyers for its product, and it pays accordingly."*

## The story and the numbers

### Origins (2015-2017) — slow and entirely manual
- Founded **2015 at the University of Michigan** by **Alex Lieberman** (a finance senior with two
  classes left, heading to Morgan Stanley as a desk trader) and **Austin Rief** (two years behind).
- Originally **"Market Corner" — a PDF attached to an email**, summarizing the Wall Street Journal
  for finance students preparing for interviews.
- Budget: **~$100/month.**
- Growth method: **three-minute pitches to every business class and club on campus**, targeting
  lecture halls of 500+ rather than individuals. **Names and emails collected on paper, by hand.**
- **~1,000 subscribers** from campus. **~10,000 by 2017** — so roughly **two years to 10k.**
- Within 6-12 months they realized the audience wasn't students but **young professionals**.

### The ambassador program — three iterations before it worked
1. **Year one:** hand-picked 10-15 "impressive" ambassadors. Most were overstretched and underperformed.
2. **Year two:** opened it to everyone. They lacked the resources to manage the cohort.
3. **Year three:** a **15-20 minute application** as a filter, admitted most applicants, then
   **concentrated resources on the top 10%** — explicit power-law management.

Reward design worth noting: ambassadors reaching **25 referrals** entered a **VIP Facebook group
with daily founder access.** The scarce, motivating resource was *contact with the founders*, not money.

### "Share the Brew" — the referral engine (launched 2017)
- Drove **~30% of growth, and as much as 80% in the early days**; instrumental in scaling from
  100,000 to millions.
- **~250,000 people referred at least one person.**
- Gamified reward ladder: exclusive events at **3 referrals**, mugs and t-shirts higher up, a **home
  office makeover at 1,000 referrals**.
- **Still ~20% of signups today**, per Rief.

### Other growth inputs
- **Cross-promotion swaps with similarly-sized newsletters** — free, and it leveraged existing
  reading habits rather than trying to change behavior.
- Once profitable, **paid acquisition became ~30% of new subscribers** (Facebook, TikTok, YouTube) —
  described as "adding fuel to the fire," notably *after* profitability, not before.
- **Open rates ~42% early, now 50%+** — roughly double the industry norm.

### Revenue timeline
| Year | Revenue |
|---|---|
| 2017 | First revenue, "very small" |
| 2018 | "A couple of million" |
| 2019 | **$13M** |
| 2020 | **$20M** |
| 2021 | **~$50M** |
| 2024 | B2B division alone **$25M** |
| 2025 | **$70M+**, double-digit profit margins |

- **Early ad rates: "a couple of hundred dollars at most."**
- **Advertising is "nearly all" of revenue.** Rief explicitly rejected subscriptions as a goal:
  *"we're not saying, hey, we want a subscription — how can we get there? We're saying, what's the
  best product and service for our users?"*
- **Covid:** the ad downturn lasted **~3 months** and they **avoided layoffs**. Rief's explanation:
  *"when companies get scared, they keep their dollars in performance advertising"* — and Morning
  Brew's ads performed as direct response, not just brand awareness.

### The exit and after
- **October 2020: Insider (Axel Springer) acquired a majority stake at a ~$75M valuation** — at the
  time the largest newsletter acquisition ever. Lieberman became executive chairman, Rief CEO.
  Axel Springer later took full ownership.
- **Headcount: ~60 → 110-120 (2021) → ~230**, with 180 hired after the sale.
- **4M+ subscribers** on the flagship.
- Now **~20 franchise brands**: Marketing Brew, Retail Brew, IT Brew, **CFO Brew (2022)**, Tech Brew,
  HR Brew, Healthcare Brew, and a slate of podcasts.
- **Multimedia (podcasts, creator-led content) pacing at "well over" $10M in 2025.**

### How the B2B verticals were built — the mechanic worth stealing
They **segmented the existing consumer list** and moved matching readers into tailored newsletters:
*a reader of the daily brew showing signs of working in finance gets pointed toward CFO Brew.*

**Only ~3 of roughly 12 B2B ad products are tied directly to newsletters.** The rest is lead
generation, webinars, and events. The newsletter is the audience-assembly mechanism; the money is
made on adjacent products sold to that audience.

## What's useful for this project

**1. ⚠️ It is the least accessible model we have studied, not the most.** Morning Brew substituted
**distribution and scale** for expertise — classroom pitching, an ambassador program, a referral
engine, paid acquisition, and eventually 230 employees. **Distribution at that intensity is exactly
what a solo operator at 5-10 hrs/week cannot supply.** The absence of an expertise requirement makes
it sound more accessible than the expert-led models. The opposite is true.

**2. It resolves the expertise question rather than contradicting it.** Combining this with Poyar,
Lenny, and Fuller:

> **Expertise is what lets you monetize a small audience. Without it, you need a very large one.**

Poyar: 85,000 subscribers → ~$1.5M. Morning Brew: 4,000,000 subscribers → ~$70M. Per subscriber
that's **~$17.50 vs ~$17.50** — remarkably similar — but one is reachable by one person working
evenings and the other required a decade and hundreds of staff. **The tension I flagged after the
first three sources is now resolved: both routes work, but they demand completely different inputs.**

**3. Consumer ad rates are structurally low, and Morning Brew's own trajectory proves it.** Early ads
went for "a couple of hundred dollars." The company reached ~$50M only at millions of subscribers,
then **pivoted toward B2B for margin.** For our purposes: **a consumer newsletter is a volume
business; a professional one is a margin business.** This is now supported by the beehiiv vertical
data, the Poyar/Imran comparison, *and* Morning Brew's own strategic shift. **Three independent
confirmations — the strongest pattern in the research so far.**

**4. The founders were their own audience, which substituted for expertise.** Lieberman was a
finance senior writing for finance students. Not deep domain expertise, but **audience proximity** —
he knew exactly what his reader wanted because he *was* the reader. That is a real and cheaper
alternative to authority, and worth carrying into the Phase 4 rubric as its own qualifying criterion.

**5. Newsletter as audience-assembly, monetized adjacently.** Only 3 of ~12 B2B ad products are
newsletter placements; the rest is lead gen, webinars, and events. **This is Fuller's owned-audience
thesis again**, arrived at independently from the consumer side — and it's now our **fourth
independent route to "own the audience, monetize adjacently."**

**6. The referral mechanic is genuinely cheap and testable.** 30% of growth (80% early) from
"Share the Brew," and the best reward at the ambassador tier wasn't money but **access to the
founders**. For a small operator, access is the one reward that costs nothing to manufacture and
cannot be commoditized.

**7. Growth was slow and unglamorous at the start.** Two years of manual work to reach 10,000
subscribers, by two people pitching lecture halls and writing names on paper. **This matches Poyar's
"glacial" 18 months to 5,000.** The slow-start pattern is now consistent across every case we have.

## Caveats

- **⚠️ Compiled from secondary sources.** Only the Press Gazette interview is founder-primary.
  Much of the tactical detail comes from referral-software and newsletter-SaaS blogs retelling the
  same anecdotes to sell a product. **The revenue figures are well corroborated across trade press;
  the growth stories are not independently verified.**
- **Timing is doing enormous unacknowledged work.** Founded 2015, scaled 2017-2020 — before the
  newsletter market was saturated, when Facebook acquisition was cheap and inbox competition was
  thin. **The same playbook run today faces a completely different market**, and none of the
  retrospectives account for this.
- **Two founders, full-time, from day one** — plus an unpaid ambassador army. Not a solo project,
  and never was.
- **The $75M figure is a valuation on a majority stake**, not a clean sale price, and the founders'
  actual proceeds are not public.
- **The consumer flagship's growth has flattened** — 4M subscribers in 2022, still ~4M now, with
  growth and margin coming from B2B and multimedia instead. **The consumer newsletter is arguably
  the mature, low-growth part of the business.**
- **Survivorship, at scale.** Morning Brew is the case everyone cites *because* it worked. The
  campus-ambassador-plus-referral playbook has been copied constantly since; we have no data on the
  failure rate, and that missing denominator is exactly what the beehiiv 0.62% median hints at.
- **⚠️ Unresolved:** Fuller praised Morning Brew for *not* doing original reporting, framing the
  derivative product as more profitable than the source. Nothing found here confirms or denies that
  claim from Morning Brew's side. **Their editorial cost structure remains unstudied**, and it's the
  crux of whether "rewrite others' reporting" is genuinely a viable model or just a cheap start.
