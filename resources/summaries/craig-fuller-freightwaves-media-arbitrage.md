## What it claims

The core thesis: **media businesses are the biggest arbitrage of the coming decade, because
the market prices them as ad-and-subscription businesses when their real asset is an owned
audience that can be monetized through adjacent commerce.**

Fuller's framing is "**negative CAC**" (his coined term). Most companies *rent* their audience
from Google, Meta, or LinkedIn, paying per lead forever. If instead you own a media property,
the advertising and subscription revenue *pays for* the audience — and you can then sell that
audience high-value adjacent products. Customer acquisition stops being a cost line and starts
contributing profit.

He argues investors have written off media (BuzzFeed, Vice) because those were platform-
dependent traffic businesses — Facebook's algorithm gave them scale and then took it away.
That failure, he says, tarred a whole asset class where durable niche properties with genuinely
committed audiences now trade at 2-5x EBITDA with no competing bidders.

## Specific numbers and facts

### FreightWaves (B2B data + media for freight/logistics)
- **Positioning:** "the Bloomberg of freight" — high-frequency supply-chain data plus editorial.
- **2019 revenue:** $11M, of which **$5.5M (50%) was in-person events** — which went to zero in 2020.
- **2020 revenue:** ~$18M, *despite* losing the entire events line.
- **Revenue grew 4x during covid; headcount grew ~10%** — 150 employees (Jan 2020) to 162.
- **~400 employees** total across FreightWaves + Flying Media Group at time of recording.
- **Total equity raised: $65M.** Explicitly less than supply-chain peers; he frames this as an advantage.
- Built the data business to **~$10M ARR with zero third-party paid advertising** — all inbound from their own media.
- First institutional round: Fontinalis Partners (Bill Ford's mobility fund, ~$300M AUM), 2017.
  Took **~a year and hundreds of pitches**; repeatedly told the idea was "a boring niche."
- **Near-death, March 2020:** roadshow timed for Q1 2020, cash runway to May, VC market froze.
  **Laid off 30% of staff in early March.** Then March 2020 became the best month in company
  history (FEMA contract + supply-chain panic). The board considered taking a PPP loan unethical
  given the reversal.

### Flying Magazine (the model case study)
- Founded **1927**. At acquisition: **106,000 subscribers**, ~**500,000 pageviews/month**.
  Purchase price: "a couple million dollars."
- **Print had lost money every single month since 2006.**
- The intervention:
  - Raised average subscription yield from **$8/year to a $30/year minimum (~4x)**.
  - Raised advertising rates simultaneously.
  - Upgraded print quality to "coffee table ready" — deliberately an art object.
- **Result: subscribers fell 106,000 → 32,000 (-70%).** Advertisers pushed back hard. For six
  months he believed he had destroyed the business.
- **Then it turned:** after the third issue, advertisers returned; **Q4 grew 260% year-over-year**
  and subscriptions began recovering.
- The pitch to advertisers: *"They don't want to reach 106,000 subscribers, they want to reach the
  100 prospective buyers of that airplane."* Aircraft advertised run $1M-$75M. Anyone unwilling to
  pay $30/year is, by definition, not a buyer — **the price increase is the qualification filter.**

### The adjacent-commerce flywheel built on Flying's audience
| Business | Acquisition / cost | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Fly-in residential community | 1,500 acres East Tennessee farmland at **$2,400/acre** | Lots at **$150,000**; acre lots **$600,000**. **Millions in reservations within ~6 months, pre-construction**, advertised in his own magazine |
| Aviation e-commerce (custom swag) | **$10,000** on Acquire.com; had done $6,000 revenue in its entire history | **~$80,000 revenue** the following year. Run with his 16-year-old son |
| Used-aircraft marketplaces | ~8 sites acquired and consolidated | Now the **#2 used-aircraft marketplace**; built a pricing estimator ("the Zestimate for airplanes") |
| Aircraft financing | **~$140,000** for a business doing ~$140,000/year | Six months later, **December alone did ~$85,000** — roughly a **$1M run rate**. Commissions average **$6,000-7,000**, some **$30,000** |

### Acquisition economics for legacy magazines
- **2-5x EBITDA**, deals largely **uncontested** — no private equity at the table, closes fast.
- The media revenue (ads + subs) **finances the acquisition**; the audience is the actual asset.
- He is now the largest aviation publisher *and* largest recreational marine publisher
  (Yachting, Sailing World, Cruising World — Yachting dating to 1907).

### Why the legacy magazine model broke ("rate base")
Publishers historically inflated subscriber counts — PTA fundraiser forms, credit-card sign-up
bundles, door-to-door sales — because advertising CPM was priced off raw subscriber count.
Those subscribers didn't care about the content. Marketers eventually caught on, and the
inflated-rate-base model collapsed. Fuller's inversion: **shrink the list to people who
demonstrably care, and charge advertisers for qualification rather than reach.**

### Other businesses cited
- **Industry Dive** — vertical B2B newsletters (Utility Dive, Waste Dive) doing original and
  roundup reporting, **no paywall**. Sold to Informa for **$500M ≈ 4x revenue, 12-15x EBITDA.**
- **Morning Brew / The Hustle** — **$80-100M revenue** built substantially on *rewriting* others'
  reporting rather than original journalism. Original reporting is expensive; the derivative
  product can be more profitable than the source. Note: he also flags that when HubSpot bought
  The Hustle, the founder left and the media property withered — a recurring failure pattern.
- **New York Times** — the most successful subscription newspaper; pivoted from advertising to
  subscriptions and **writes deliberately for its subscribers**. Polarization is a *feature*:
  Fox does the same thing from the opposite direction. His claim: media businesses die from
  being neutral and therefore boring.
- **The Surfer's Journal / The Golfer's Journal** — cited as models of the magazine-as-art-object.
- **BuzzFeed / Vice** — the cautionary case. Grew by hacking the Facebook algorithm; when
  Zuckerberg cut off referral traffic, the model collapsed. "A one-trick pony."
- **Convoy** — among the largest VC-backed failures ever; raised vastly more than FreightWaves.

## What's useful for this project

**1. There is a fifth monetization model we hadn't scoped.** PROJECT.md lists ads, subscriptions,
sponsorships, products, and audience-as-funnel. Fuller's model is distinct: *acquire or build an
audience, then buy adjacent businesses that sell to it.* The media property is the customer
acquisition engine and the operating businesses are where the margin is. This deserves explicit
treatment in Phase 2.

**2. Audience quality beats audience size, and price is the filter.** The Flying case is the
cleanest demonstration in the interview: deliberately destroying 70% of the subscriber list made
the business work. A small list that pays is worth more than a large one that doesn't — both to
you and to advertisers.

**3. Firsthand practitioner knowledge is the wedge.** FreightWaves' media breakout came from
Fuller writing about hurricane logistics from direct FEMA experience while a hired journalist was
on vacation — traffic went from ~40,000 pageviews/month to individual articles doing hundreds of
thousands. His framing: the Bloomberg/ESPN model, where practitioners cover their own field, beats
journalists interviewing sources two weeks late. **This is the strongest argument in the interview
for Track A (domain edge) over Track B (open market scan).**

**4. Boring B2B niches are structurally advantaged.** Repeatedly dismissed as "a boring niche" by
investors — which is precisely why it was uncontested. High-value B2B verticals have advertisers
with real budgets and readers whose employers will expense a subscription. Industry Dive's $500M
exit is the proof point.

**5. Strong opinions are a business model, not a personality flaw.** NYT and Fox as structurally
identical: pick an audience, write for it, accept alienating everyone else. Neutrality reads as
boring, and boring doesn't retain.

**6. Niche discipline applies to the personal account too.** His PR director profiled every tweet:
supply-chain and media posts drove high engagement, off-topic posts (college football) drove zero
*and actively diluted the brand*. He also frames Twitter/X as a free lab for learning media
mechanics — the engagement rules transfer.

**7. Small, low-consequence bets are the generating function.** The media arm was not in the seed
deck. The futures market — the actual founding thesis — traded roughly 30 times in a year and was
a total failure. The conference series came from a blockchain consortium he admits he created as a
pretext to call executives, expecting 30 attendees and getting 150.

## Caveats

- **Every figure is unverified founder self-report** on a two-hour promotional podcast. The host is
  a VC and friendly throughout; nothing is challenged.
- **Talking his own book.** He is actively acquiring magazines. "This asset class is underpriced
  and nobody else is bidding" is exactly what an active acquirer says.
- **Severe survivorship bias.** We hear about Flying working. We don't hear the base rate — how
  many magazine acquisitions of this type fail, or what happened to properties he passed on. He
  concedes the futures market failed, which is a point in his favor, but the failures discussed are
  all *inside* a company that succeeded overall.
- **⚠️ This is a capital-intensive playbook and largely out of reach at 5-10 hrs/week.** Flying cost
  "a couple million dollars"; the land purchase was ~$3.6M at the quoted per-acre price; he had
  already had a successful company and had "fired himself from every role" before starting it. The
  *principles* transfer to a small operator — own the audience, qualify over scale, sell adjacent —
  but the *mechanics* (acquisition, roll-up, 400 employees) do not. Do not let the framing smuggle
  in a strategy the capacity budget can't support.
- **Timing was a huge and acknowledged input.** FreightWaves' breakout was covid making supply
  chains a boardroom topic. Fuller says outright that "the entire story of FreightWaves is luck."
  Read the replicable mechanics, discount the outcome.
- **Data is from February 2024** — roughly 2.5 years stale. Post-2024 outcomes for FreightWaves and
  the magazine roll-up are unknown to us and worth checking before relying on any of it.
- **Two apparent errors in the transcript:** he dates the Blockchain in Transport Alliance to 2007,
  which conflicts with the surrounding 2017 narrative and with BiTA's actual founding; and several
  proper nouns are ASR-mangled. Verify names and dates before citing them anywhere.
- **Unresolved tension worth watching:** he praises Morning Brew for *not* doing original reporting
  while arguing that firsthand expertise is the durable moat. Both can't be the general rule — this
  is a real conflict to resolve in Phase 2, not a throwaway.
